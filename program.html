Day 1

Self-awareness and personal wellbeing

Guiding question
How do I understand myself, sustain my wellbeing, and recognize my influence on others?
Purpose
How do my values, habits, and emotional responses shape leadership?

The day places the wellbeing and personal growth of Deans and Senior faculty at the center of leadership development. Participants bring their values, habits, aspirations, and pressures into their roles at home, at work, and in the community. They will examine how these shape their responses, relationships, and use of authority. This provides a foundation for understanding how their wellbeing and conduct influence colleagues, staff, students, and institutional culture.

Question to carry forward

“What do I need to understand or change in myself to care for my wellbeing and exercise my responsibilities with care?”

The day, session by session
Opening

Opening and purpose

Begin with how understandings of wellbeing extend beyond illness, academic achievement and professional success. Connect global and Indian concerns with the experiences of higher education institutions.

Reflection

Understanding myself across my roles

Participants map their roles within families, institutions and communities. Through reflection and paired conversation, they examine their values, strengths, limitations and leadership styles. They consider where their actions reflect their values, where responsibilities conflict and how their position influences their expectations of others.

Wellbeing

Understanding and sustaining my wellbeing

Explore emotional awareness, resilience, self-motivation, time and priorities. Introduce autonomy, competence and relatedness as a framework for understanding wellbeing. Participants identify sources of fulfilment and pressure, examine their responses to uncertainty and setbacks, and distinguish changes within their control from conditions requiring support or institutional action.

Lunch
Role play

How do others experience me?

Through role reversal and scenarios, participants examine the gap between intention and impact. Situations include responding to a family member’s mistake, handling a staff member’s request, receiving criticism from a colleague, explaining an administrative decision or addressing a student’s missed deadline. The focus is on recognising assumptions, emotional triggers and habits in exercising authority.

Mapping

My wellbeing and the environments I help create

Participants map the relationships and institutional practices that support or strain their wellbeing. They then examine practices they influence allocating work, setting deadlines, conducting meetings, granting permissions, evaluating performance or responding to requests. Discussion connects their own need for trust, fairness and support with the experiences of colleagues, staff and students.

Commitment

Reflection and a first commitment

Prepare a personal wellbeing map identifying one habit to change, one relationship to attend to and one condition for which support is needed. Participants also identify one practice within their institutional role that they can change to support their own and others’ wellbeing.

Evening

A campus walk with students

Student partners guide participants through learning, residential, administrative, and social spaces. Teams trace an everyday journey, such as seeking an extension or finding support, and notice where signage, access, language, or behavior creates friction.

Day 2

Communication, relationships and teamwork

Guiding question
How can I listen, express myself, and work with others in ways that support our wellbeing?
Purpose

The day focuses on how Deans and Senior faculty communicate and build relationships. Participants will examine how they express expectations, respond to disagreement, give feedback, and share responsibility. Participants will connect their own experiences of being heard, trusted, or excluded with the environments they create. Within institutions, differences in authority can influence who speaks, whose concerns receive attention, and whether people seek support.

Question to carry forward

“How can I make room for others’ voices while expressing my own needs and fulfilling my responsibilities?”

The day, session by session
Practice

Listening and expressing myself

Through paired exercises and demonstrations, participants practise active listening, empathy, clear expression, constructive feedback and respectful disagreement. They notice habits such as interrupting, assuming, avoiding a concern or offering advice before understanding it. Examples span family conversations, workplace exchanges and interactions with students.

Group task

Building trust and working through differences

A collaborative task reveals patterns in participation, decision-making and shared responsibility. Participants examine how they recognise others’ strengths, respond to competing views and negotiate expectations. They practise conflict resolution while attending to dignity, fairness and relationships.

Lunch
Practice

Conversations that matter

In groups of three, participants take turns as speaker, listener and observer. Scenarios include negotiating responsibilities at home, discussing workload with a supervisor, addressing a colleague’s conduct, responding to a staff member’s concern or explaining a decision to a student. After feedback, participants repeat the conversation using a different approach and reflect on what changes.

Role play

Authority, voice and belonging

Through role reversal, participants explore how hierarchy, subtle discrimination and intimidation shape communication. They practise inviting disagreement, responding to exclusion, setting boundaries and acknowledging mistakes. A rewriting exercise turns a dismissive email or administrative notice into a message that explains the decision, recognises its impact and provides a route for questions or assistance.

Commitment

Reflection and a relationship commitment

Participants identify one communication habit to change and prepare for a conversation they have been postponing. They outline what they want to express, what they need to understand and a possible next step. Each participant also proposes one team practice—such as agreeing on responsibilities or inviting input before decisions—to improve participation and trust.

Evening

Screening of 12 Angry Men

Screening of 12 Angry Men, followed by a discussion on listening, dissent, and changing one’s mind. Participants consider: Who makes it easier for others to speak? When does persuasion become pressure? How do we respond when someone challenges our position?

Day 3

Critical thinking and decision-making

Guiding question
How can I examine my assumptions and make decisions that account for evidence, uncertainty, and their effects on others?
Purpose

The day invites Deans and Senior faculty to examine how they interpret situations, form judgments, and respond to uncertainty. Participants will explore how expectations, emotions, habits, and institutional roles influence decisions across their personal and professional lives.

The connection with wellbeing lies in how people experience these judgments - decisions about responsibilities, opportunities, performance, or access to support affect colleagues, staff, and students. Participants will practice considering different perspectives, explaining their reasoning, and revising a decision when evidence warrants it.

Question to carry forward

“What might I be missing, and what would give me reason to change my mind?”

The day, session by session
Reflection

Questioning my assumptions

Participants examine situations in which an initial impression may be mistaken: a family member withdrawing from conversation, a colleague missing a commitment, a staff member questioning instructions or a student disengaging. They distinguish observations from interpretations, identify assumptions and consider what further information they need.

Case

Making decisions under uncertainty

Groups work through a case involving competing needs and incomplete information. They assess evidence, seek different perspectives, develop alternatives and weigh consequences. New information is introduced midway, requiring them to reconsider their position. Discussion addresses collaboration, calculated risk and when to decide, consult or seek further evidence.

Lunch
Art

Seeing differently through art and storytelling

Using images, comics or short narratives, participants develop more than one interpretation of the same situation. They create a story from another person’s viewpoint and identify what their first account overlooked. The discussion connects interpretation with judgments about behaviour, motivation and wellbeing.

Theatre

Rehearsing choices through theatre

Participants enact a dilemma involving family expectations, workload, resource allocation or an institutional request. They pause the scene, exchange roles and try different decisions. Observers identify whose perspective is missing, how authority operates and what consequences each response may produce.

Commitment

Reflection and a decision commitment

Each participant revisits a decision they face and records its assumptions, available evidence, affected people and alternatives. They identify whom to consult, what remains uncertain and when the decision should be reviewed.

Evening

Art, theater and the experience of belonging

Participants and students make a comic, collage, or visual story about a campus encounter. Work in mixed groups with no artistic prerequisites. Discuss how drawing and making can let people communicate experiences that are difficult to express in a formal meeting.

Day 4

Ethical leadership and social responsibility

Guiding question
How can I exercise authority with fairness and contribute to the wellbeing of the communities to which I belong?
Purpose

The day connects personal values with the responsibilities of Deans and Senior faculty. Participants will examine how integrity, accountability and care appear in everyday conduct: keeping commitments, acknowledging mistakes, sharing opportunities and responding to concerns. Wellbeing is shaped by relationships, resources, social conditions and institutional practices. Participants will consider how workload, hierarchy, discrimination, financial circumstances and access to support affect their own and others’ experiences. They will explore how individual conduct and collective action can support dignity, participation and belonging.

Question to carry forward

“What am I responsible for changing through my own conduct, and what requires collective action?”

The day, session by session
Discussion

Values, authority and accountability

Participants examine dilemmas involving favouritism, confidentiality, competing obligations and pressure to overlook a concern. They consider consistency between stated values and conduct, the reasons behind their choices and their responsibility to explain decisions. Practice includes acknowledging an error and identifying steps to repair its effects.

Discussion

Fairness, belonging and social responsibility

Through cases, participants explore how power, background and access to resources shape participation. They examine subtle discrimination, exclusion and the difference between applying the same rule and responding fairly to different circumstances. Discussion extends to responsibilities towards the community and environment, and how leaders set examples through everyday choices.

Lunch · Time for rest and informal conversation.
Practice

Responding to concerns and connecting people with support

A counselling professional facilitates scenarios involving a colleague, staff member or student seeking help. Participants practise listening, recognising the limits of their role and connecting the person with support. They examine privacy, referral pathways and institutional procedures, while reflecting on their own willingness and ability to seek assistance.

Design

Designing a SafeSpace initiative

Groups develop a proposal for a space or process through which faculty, administrators, staff and students can raise concerns and access support. They specify its purpose, accessibility, facilitators, confidentiality limits, referral arrangements and accountability. They also identify institutional conditions that must change for people to trust and use it.

Commitment

Reflection and an ethical commitment

Participants identify one gap between their values and conduct, and one institutional practice they can influence. They outline an action, the people who should be involved and how those affected can provide feedback.

Evening

Screening of Judgment at Nuremberg

Screening of Judgment at Nuremberg, followed by discussion on conscience, obedience and responsibility. Participants examine the claim that following instructions removes personal responsibility, while recognizing the film’s historical context.

Day 5

Integration, commitment and institutional contribution

Guiding question
How will I translate these experiences into changes in my life, relationships and institution?
Purpose

The final day brings together self-awareness, communication, critical thinking and ethical leadership. Participants will develop commitments that support their wellbeing and improve how they relate to family members, colleagues, staff and students. The emphasis is on changes that can be sustained within participants’ responsibilities and circumstances. Personal commitments will be connected with institutional action, recognizing that wellbeing requires both individual agency and supportive conditions. Participants will consider the relationships, practices, and capacity they want to leave behind.

Question to carry forward

“What will people experience differently because of what I do after this program?”

The day, session by session
Synthesis

Bringing the learning together

Groups address a staged institutional case involving workload pressure, a breakdown in communication, a disputed decision and a wellbeing concern. As information emerges, participants apply the four course sections: recognising their responses, listening to others, examining evidence and exercising responsibility. The debrief considers both immediate action and underlying institutional conditions.

Plan

Developing a 90-day action plan

Each participant develops commitments in three areas: personal wellbeing, a relationship or team practice, and an institutional process they can influence. Plans specify a first-week action, a 30-day milestone and a 90-day review, alongside responsibilities, support required and evidence of progress. Participants distinguish actions within their authority from those requiring collaboration or approval.

Lunch · Time for rest and informal conversation.
Feedback

Testing plans with those affected

Participants present their plans to peers and invited representatives from faculty, administration, staff and students. Feedback addresses whether the proposed changes meet a need, account for different experiences and create unintended burdens. Participants revise their plans in response.

Plan

Building relationships and practices that last

Groups examine how changes can continue beyond an individual’s tenure. They consider mentoring, sharing responsibility, documenting practices, building relationships across institutional roles and creating routes for feedback. Each participant identifies one contribution they want to make to the institution’s future.

Commitment

Commitment, support and follow-through

Participants share the commitments they choose to make public and form peer partnerships for follow-up. They agree on check-in dates and how to review progress, including feedback from people affected by their actions. A closing reflection revisits the question from Day 1: how do I understand myself, sustain my wellbeing and recognise my influence on others?

Closing activity

Note to future selves

Participants write a note to their future selves describing what they want to remember, practice, and reconsider. They retain it for their 90-day review.
